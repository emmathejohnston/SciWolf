## OPTICS

### Lesson O1: "Why Do Things Have Colour, and How Does Light Travel?"
**Combines:** EM spectrum + colour/absorption/reflection/transmission + types of light emission
**Anchor phenomena:** A green leaf, a neon sign, a glow-in-the-dark sticker
**Arc:**
1. Students classify each phenomenon by how it interacts with light (absorption, reflection, transmission) and locate it on the EM spectrum
2. Students classify the emission type for each object (incandescence, fluorescence, bioluminescence, etc.)
3. Scaffolded wavelength/frequency/energy GRASS calculation (numbers provided, structure scaffolded)
4. Closing prompt: "Design a light source" for a given scenario (the life raft scenario from the unit test works well here)

**Deliverable:** Google Form
**Est. time:** 70–80 min
**SciWolf alignment:** No current sim covers EM spectrum or emission types. A future "EM Spectrum Explorer" or colour mixing tool would slot in here naturally. For now, this lesson is fully self-contained on the HTML page.

### Lesson O2: "Which Mirror Should We Use?"
**Combines:** Plane mirrors + curved mirrors + real-world application
**Arc:**
1. Guided LOST example for a plane mirror, then two independent LOST descriptions (diagrams provided as images — students describe, not draw)
2. Structured comparison of all three mirror types across object positions
3. Three real design scenarios (makeup mirror, convenience store security mirror, car side mirror) — students recommend, justify, and describe the image using LOST

**Deliverable:** Google Doc
**Est. time:** 75–85 min
**SciWolf alignment:** ⭐ STRONG — Ray Diagram Simulator (sciwolf.com/raydiagram.html) handles plane, concave, and convex mirrors with draggable object position, live LOST-relevant output, and principal ray display. This upgrades the lesson significantly: rather than describing static images, students drag the object to prescribed positions in the sim and record what they observe. The lesson arc can be restructured around the sim as the primary tool, with the Google Doc capturing their observations and reasoning. Recommend embedding the sim directly in the lesson page or linking prominently. The "describe, don't draw" design decision can be relaxed — the sim handles the drawing, students handle the thinking.
**Notes:** Original design had students describing static diagrams — with the SciWolf sim, this becomes a genuine interactive inquiry. Supply teacher viability is maintained since the sim is self-guiding.

### Lesson O3: "Why Does Light Bend, and What Can We Do With It?"
**Combines:** Refraction rules + total internal reflection + lenses + eye correction
**Anchor phenomenon:** A straw in a glass of water
**Arc:**
1. Snell's Law — two scaffolded GRASS problems (air-to-water, water-to-glass)
2. Embedded PhET "Bending Light" simulation — students increase angle of incidence to discover TIR, answer structured observation questions
3. Myopic vs. hyperopic eye diagrams — students explain the refractive error, select the correct lens, complete one thin-lens GRASS calculation (scaffolded)

**Deliverable:** Google Form
**Est. time:** 75–90 min (PhET section variable)
**SciWolf alignment:** PARTIAL — Ray Diagram Simulator (sciwolf.com/raydiagram.html) covers the lens section well: students can explore converging/diverging lenses, drag object positions, and verify their thin-lens calculations against the sim's live output. This is genuinely useful for Section 3 (eye correction + calculation). However, the sim does not currently handle refraction at a boundary or TIR — so PhET "Bending Light" remains the best tool for Section 2. Consider structuring the lesson so SciWolf handles lenses and PhET handles refraction/TIR, making this a two-sim lesson. A future SciWolf refraction sim would allow you to drop the PhET dependency entirely.
**Notes:** No custom simulation needed — PhET "Bending Light" is a free embed. This is the most calculation-heavy lesson in the set.

## BIOLOGY

### Lesson B1: "Why Are Cells the Way They Are?"
**Combines:** Cell structure + organelle function + surface area:volume ratio
**Anchor question:** "Why aren't cells the size of your fist?"
**Arc:**
1. Students calculate SA:V for three cube sizes (scaffolded table), connect to diffusion efficiency
2. "Spot the Mistake" — 6 intentionally incorrect cell diagrams or descriptions; students identify, explain, and correct each error
3. Closing prediction: given a specific cell type (e.g., muscle cell, root hair cell), students predict which organelles would be most abundant and why

**Deliverable:** Google Doc
**Est. time:** 75–80 min
**SciWolf alignment:** PARTIAL — Inner Life of a Cell (sciwolf.com/cell.html) is engaging and relevant but doesn't map cleanly onto the lesson arc. The game (you are a mitochondrion, donate ATP to organelles) could work as a 5-minute hook at the start to orient students to organelle locations and functions before the SA:V calculation and Spot the Mistake sections. It's better used as an appetizer than a core tool here. A future sim — a labelled cell diagram with clickable organelles showing function, or a plant vs. animal cell comparison tool — would serve B1 much more directly.
**Notes:** The "Spot the Mistake" section is the most inquiry-rich part and works well without teacher presence.

### Lesson B2: "What Is Mitosis Actually For, and What Happens When It Goes Wrong?"
**Combines:** Cell cycle + mitosis phases + cancer + stem cells
**Anchor scenario:** A patient has been diagnosed with a tumour — what does that mean at the cellular level?
**Arc:**
1. Students order a scrambled mitosis sequence (images on website) and justify each placement using phase descriptions — no drawing required. Optional: embed PhET "Cell Division"
2. Structured comparison of cancerous vs. normal cell division — focus on *why* differences matter (DNA damage, checkpoints, apoptosis)
3. Short ethical scenario: a doctor recommends a stem cell treatment — students explain what stem cells are, why they might help, and identify one legitimate concern

**Deliverable:** Google Form
**Est. time:** 75–85 min
**SciWolf alignment:** WEAK currently — no mitosis sim exists on SciWolf. Inner Life of a Cell (sciwolf.com/cell.html) touches adjacent content but doesn't address the cell cycle or division phases. PhET "Cell Division" remains the best available option for Section 1. A mitosis sequencing tool on SciWolf — drag-and-drop phase ordering with labelled cell images — would be high value here and is probably the single highest-priority future sim for the biology unit.
**Notes:**

### Lesson B3: "Trace the Cheese Sandwich / What's Wrong with Karen?"
**Combines:** Digestive + circulatory + respiratory systems → cellular respiration; organ system malfunction diagnosis
**Arc:**
1. *Trace the Cheese Sandwich* (35–40 min): Students trace a cheese sandwich through digestive, circulatory, and respiratory systems to a muscle cell making ATP. Website provides a partially completed flowchart; students fill in missing steps and substances, then write a short narrative summary.
2. *What's Wrong with Karen?* (35–40 min): Karen presents with shortness of breath, low oxygen saturation, and fatigue. Students identify the affected system, name the likely tissue/organ, explain the mechanism, and recommend a next step.

**Deliverable:** Google Doc
**Est. time:** 75–85 min
**SciWolf alignment:** WEAK currently — no organ systems sim exists on SciWolf. Inner Life of a Cell (sciwolf.com/cell.html) could serve as a brief entry point for the cellular respiration endpoint of the cheese sandwich trace (students visit the sim to see the mitochondrion's role before writing their narrative), but this is a loose connection. A future circulatory or digestive system flow diagram sim would anchor B3 directly. For now, the lesson is fully self-contained on the HTML page with a static flowchart.
**Notes:** The two halves reinforce each other — the first builds the map, the second requires students to break it. Natural transition point if a class runs differently.

## CHEMISTRY

### Lesson C1: "Why Do Atoms Bond, and What Kind of Bond Is It?"
**Combines:** Ionic bonding + molecular bonding + naming + formulas
**Anchor phenomenon:** Sodium explodes in water — but sodium chloride is table salt. Why?
**Arc:**
1. Electron transfer logic for ionic bonding (Lewis dot scaffolded); electron sharing logic for molecular bonding
2. Given 6–8 compounds (mix of ionic, molecular, polyatomic) — students classify, name, and write the formula
3. "Explain it to Karen" closing prompt: Karen thinks ionic and molecular compounds are basically the same thing. Write her a correction using at least two specific differences.

**Deliverable:** Google Form
**Est. time:** 70–80 min
**SciWolf alignment:** ⭐ STRONG — Bohr-Rutherford Diagram (sciwolf.com/bohr.html) is a direct fit for Section 1. Students build the atoms for an ionic pair (e.g., sodium + fluorine) in the sim, observe valence electrons live, then use that as the basis for drawing the Lewis dot bonding process in their deliverable. The sim shows both Bohr-Rutherford and Lewis dot views simultaneously, which is exactly the scaffolding students need before working through electron transfer and sharing logic on their own. Recommend embedding or prominently linking the sim at the start of Section 1 with explicit instructions (e.g., "Build sodium. How many valence electrons does it have? Now build fluorine.").
**Notes:** The compound list should be kept to 6 to avoid running long. Denser than other lessons — scaffolding on Lewis dot section is critical.

---

### Lesson C2: "What's Actually Happening in This Reaction?"
**Combines:** Reaction types + balancing equations + conservation of mass
**Anchor:** Four short embedded video clips (precipitation, combustion, fizzing, displacement)
**Arc:**
1. Students classify each video reaction by type
2. For each reaction: write the word equation → skeletal equation → balanced equation (scaffolded progressively — first one mostly done, last one independent)
3. Conservation of mass twist: a reaction *appears* to lose mass (open system, gas escapes) — students explain why without violating the law
4. Closing: "Karen and the furnace filter" prompt (incomplete combustion + real consequences)

**Deliverable:** Google Doc
**Est. time:** 80–90 min
**SciWolf alignment:** ⭐ STRONG — Chemical Equation Balancer (sciwolf.com/equationbalancer.html) maps directly onto Section 2. The three difficulty levels (Mild, Medium, Spicy) align well with the lesson's progressive scaffolding, and the live atom-count table gives students immediate feedback without needing a teacher present. Recommended use: after students classify each reaction type and write the word/skeletal equation on the Google Doc, they verify and practice the balancing step using the sim at the appropriate difficulty level. The "Spicy" level covers combustion, which ties directly to the furnace filter closing prompt. Note that the sim generates randomised equations rather than the specific ones in the lesson — this is fine for the balancing practice section but means the Google Doc needs to carry the classification and word equation work independently.
**Notes:** Most cognitively demanding lesson in the set. Consider a "choose 3 of 4 reactions" option to manage time. Scaffolding on the balancing section is critical.

### Lesson C3: "Neutralize the Spill / Is the Soil Safe?"
**Combines:** pH scale + acid/base properties + neutralization reactions
**Anchor:** Two parallel scenarios — a NaOH spill in a school lab, and farm soil with pH 4.2
**Arc:**
1. Students classify substances by pH, identify acid/base properties, determine what type each scenario represents
2. For each scenario: identify an appropriate neutralizing substance, write the word equation and balanced chemical equation, predict the pH outcome
3. "Advise the farmer" closing prompt: the farmer wants to add acidic fertilizer to already-acidic soil — students explain the problem and recommend a solution using neutralization terminology

**Deliverable:** Google Form
**Est. time:** 70–80 min
**SciWolf alignment:** ⭐ STRONG — pH Indicator Lab (sciwolf.com/ph.html) fits Section 1 directly. Rather than being told the pH of the spill scenarios, students can drag the relevant substances into wells, apply indicators, and use the digital pH meter to discover the values themselves — which makes the classification step genuinely inquiry-based rather than just reading comprehension. The [H⁺]/[OH⁻] calculator is a useful extension for students who finish early. One honest gap: the sim doesn't simulate mixing an acid and base together, so the neutralization equation writing in Section 2 remains paper/form-based. That's fine — the sim earns its place in Section 1.
**Notes:** ✅ **BUILT** — `lesson-c3.html`. Print-to-PDF deliverable (no Google Form). 47 autosaved fields. Linked from `standalone.html`. Google Form link placeholder at bottom — swap in when form is created.

## CLIMATE

### Lesson CL1: "Why Is Venus 465°C?"
**Combines:** Natural greenhouse effect + enhanced greenhouse effect + greenhouse gases + atmosphere/hydrosphere
**Anchor data:** Earth avg 15°C, Venus avg 465°C, Mars avg -60°C — all receive similar solar energy
**Arc:**
1. Students work through the greenhouse mechanism using the Venus/Mars comparison — makes the natural effect concrete before introducing human-caused enhancement
2. Students analyze the Keeling Curve and one Arctic ice extent dataset (embedded as images or iframes) — describe the trend, identify the timeframe, connect to a mechanism
3. Structured comparison of natural vs. anthropogenic greenhouse effect — students identify three specific human activities, the gases produced, and the mechanism by which those gases trap heat

**Deliverable:** Google Doc
**Est. time:** 70–80 min
**SciWolf alignment:** NONE currently. Climate is the biggest gap on SciWolf. A future "Greenhouse Effect" or "Earth Energy Budget" visualisation — even a simple one showing incoming solar radiation, surface absorption, and re-emission with adjustable CO₂ — would anchor CL1 directly and make the Venus/Earth/Mars comparison interactive rather than static. High priority for future development.

### Lesson CL2: "How Do We Actually Know?"
**Combines:** Evidence of climate change + atmosphere/hydrosphere mechanisms + data literacy
**Anchor question:** A skeptic asks: "How do we actually know the climate is changing?"
**Arc:**
1. Four real datasets presented cleanly on the website: Keeling Curve, NASA GISS global temperature anomaly, Arctic sea ice extent, sea level rise. For each: describe the trend, identify the rate of change, connect to a specific atmospheric or hydrospheric mechanism.
2. "Respond to the claim" section: three short skeptic statements (e.g., "Climate has always changed naturally") — students respond using specific evidence from the datasets

**Deliverable:** Google Form
**Est. time:** 75–85 min
**SciWolf alignment:** NONE currently. This lesson is fully self-contained using embedded publicly available datasets. A future SciWolf data explorer for climate datasets could upgrade this lesson — but it's not needed to make the lesson work. Of all the lessons in the set, this one is least dependent on simulation.
**Notes:** Most transferable critical thinking lesson in the set. Works well without any science background from the supply teacher. The datasets are all publicly available and embeddable.

### Lesson CL3: "Does This Initiative Actually Work?"
**Combines:** Climate initiatives + greenhouse gas mechanisms + evidence evaluation
**Three initiatives:** Carbon pricing (Canada's carbon tax — locally relevant), reforestation (e.g., Trillion Trees), electric vehicles
**Arc:**
1. For each initiative: What's the mechanism? Which greenhouse gases does it address? What does the evidence say? What are the limitations? (Structured evaluation table)
2. Closing recommendation: students write a short memo to a fictional city council choosing between two of the three initiatives — requires synthesis, not just summary

**Deliverable:** Google Doc
**Est. time:** 75–80 min
**SciWolf alignment:** NONE currently. No sim on SciWolf touches policy or initiative evaluation, nor should one necessarily — this lesson is more humanities-adjacent than the others. Self-contained on the HTML page.
**Notes:** More "analyze and evaluate" than true inquiry — appropriate for a supply day context. The recommendation prompt pushes toward Exceeding descriptors in the rubric.

## CROSS-CUTTING NOTES

**Google Form vs. Google Doc:**
- Forms: better for structured, multi-section analytical lessons where you want consistent data and easy review (O1, O3, B2, C1, C3, CL2)
- Docs: better where students need to sketch, use tables, or write at length (O2, B1, B3, C2, CL1, CL3)

**Supply teacher viability:**
- Lessons where the website does the scaffolding (locked progression, embedded prompts) are more robust than ones that rely on reading the room
- The "Spot the Mistake" (B1) and mystery/scenario formats (B3, C3) are the most self-contained
- Avoid any lesson requiring the supply teacher to explain a concept — the website should do all of that

**PhET simulations (free, no backend):**
- O3: "Bending Light" — https://phet.colorado.edu/en/simulations/bending-light (until a SciWolf refraction sim exists)
- B2: "Cell Division" — https://phet.colorado.edu/en/simulations/cell-division (until a SciWolf mitosis sim exists)

## KEY OVERLAP: KAN-38 Phase 5 ↔ B2 Mitosis Sequencer

KAN-38 (Inner Life of a Cell revamp) already requires full mitosis animations in Phase 5 — prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase, cytokinesis, with organelle distribution to daughter cells.

The B2 lesson needs a *mitosis sequencer*: students drag phase cards into the correct order, with labelled cell images showing what the cell looks like at each phase.

These share the same underlying art: canvas drawings of a cell at each mitosis phase. Build them together:
- Phase 5 of KAN-38 builds the canvas animations
- B2's sequencer reuses those same cell drawings as static snapshots + drag-to-order interaction
- Don't build the mitosis visuals twice

Ideal sequence: finish KAN-38 Phase 5, then extract the phase images into the B2 sequencer as a follow-on task.

## ARCHITECTURE NOTE

Lesson pages are much lighter than sims — mostly HTML/CSS with embedded content. Single-file is fine for lesson pages (no game loop, no canvas physics). Multi-file architecture (as planned for KAN-38 and circuit.html) is only warranted when a lesson embeds a substantial new sim.

Exception: if CL1's greenhouse visualisation grows into a proper interactive, split it out as `greenhouse.html` and embed it in the lesson page via iframe or direct link — same pattern as the existing sim cards.
