## Ecological Info (per species)

**Lichen:** One of the first colonists of bare rock and soil. Lichens are a partnership between fungi and algae, slowly breaking down rock into soil.

**Moss:** Mosses hold moisture and create the first thin organic layer — the very beginning of soil. Hundreds of invertebrates shelter here.

**Dandelion:** An opportunist and a healer. Dandelions mine deep minerals with long taproots, and their pollen is one of the first insect foods of spring.

**Fireweed (Rosebay Willowherb):** The pioneer of burnt and disturbed ground. Fireweed can colonise a fresh clearing within weeks, its seeds carried miles on the wind.

**Common Earthball:** An early colonist of poor, disturbed soils. Unlike most mushrooms it has no real stem — a leathery sphere bursts from the ground, releasing a brown cloud of spores when it splits.

**Bunchgrass:** Grasses stabilise soil with dense fibrous roots. A single tussock may host dozens of invertebrate species.

**Yarrow:** A medicine chest in a plant. Yarrow attracts over 70 species of insect and accumulates potassium, enriching the soil when it dies.

**Blackberry:** Blackberry is simultaneously nurse, armour, and larder. Its dense thicket shelters tree seedlings from deer, while feeding birds who spread those seedlings' seeds.

**Silver Birch:** The birch is a pioneer tree — fast-growing, light-hungry, short-lived. Its roots partner with fungi and its canopy shelters the next generation of trees that will eventually replace it.

**Rowan:** The rowan is a tree of edges — found at forest margins and high altitudes where little else grows. Its berries are one of the most important foods for birds entering the hungry winter months.

**Shaggy Inkcap:** Lawyer's Wig. Pushes through trampled grassland and roadside verges. By the time you fetch a basket, the cap has begun to autodigest, releasing its spores as a slow drip of black liquid.

**Hazel:** Hazel has fed humans and animals for thousands of years. It supports over 70 species of insect. Coppiced hazel grows back vigorously and was the structural material of ancient buildings.

**Hawthorn:** One of Britain's oldest native trees, hawthorn can live for centuries. Its dense thorny growth is impenetrable to livestock but irresistible to nesting birds.

**Bracken:** Bracken is ancient — its family predates the dinosaurs by 200 million years. It covers vast areas of upland Britain, sheltering deer and small mammals.

**Foxglove:** The foxglove contains cardiac glycosides that can stop a human heart — yet the same compounds, refined, have saved millions of lives as the drug digoxin.

**Ash:** Ash was once the most common woodland tree in Britain. Now threatened by ash dieback disease, mature ash trees are becoming rare — each surviving one a small miracle.

**Common Stinkhorn:** Bursts from a soft white 'egg' in the leaf litter, growing inches per hour. Its slimy olive cap reeks of rotting flesh — a strategy that bypasses wind by recruiting flies for dispersal.

**Tinder Bracket:** Bracket fungi are decomposers — they slowly consume the heartwood of standing and fallen trees, returning centuries of accumulated carbon to the soil. Dead wood is not dead.

**Pedunculate Oak:** A single mature oak supports over 2,300 species of insect, lichen, bird, and mammal — more than any other native tree. An oak forest is not a collection of trees. It is a nation.

**Bluebell:** Half the world's bluebells grow in Britain. They appear only in spring, before the leaf canopy closes and shuts out the light — living on stored energy for the rest of the year.

**Wild Garlic:** Wild garlic — or 'ramsons' — carpets ancient woodland floors in spring. Its scent is extraordinary. Bears seek it out after winter to cleanse their digestion.

**Chanterelle:** The chanterelle mycelium lives in symbiosis with tree roots, exchanging minerals for sugars. What you see is merely the fruiting body — the organism beneath may span acres.

**Honey Fungus:** Clustered caps on the surface; underground, a single individual may stretch for kilometres of black bootlace strands, slowly killing the trees it has lived alongside for a century.

**Fly Agaric:** The fly agaric is the classic toadstool of fairy tales. It is the mycorrhizal partner of birch and pine — invisible networks connecting the roots of many trees simultaneously.

**Holly:** Holly is one of the few broadleaved evergreens in British woodland. In winter, when all other berries are gone, its red berries feed thrushes, redwings, and fieldfares.

**Ivy:** Ivy is neither parasite nor friend — it is opportunist. Its evergreen leaves provide winter cover for insects and its late flowers are the last nectar of the year, crucial for queen bumblebees preparing for winter.

**Death Cap:** The most dangerous mushroom in the world. Found in the company of mature oak and beech, exchanging minerals for sugars through a quiet underground partnership.

## Persistence Strategies

| Species | Strategy | Note |
|---|---|---|
| lichen | symbiotic | Not one organism. A fungus and an alga living as one body — a partnership older than plants. |
| moss | spore | No flowers, no seeds. Releases spores from tiny capsules — a lineage 450 million years old. |
| dandelion | clonal | Most dandelions set seed without any pollination. Each puff is largely a clone of its parent. |
| fireweed | opportunist | Seeds drift on silk for kilometres. It also spreads from underground stems — first to arrive, hard to remove. |
| grass | clonal | Runners and rhizomes spread it sideways; the flowers are almost an afterthought. |
| yarrow | clonal | Spreads by seed and by underground stems. A yarrow patch is often one slow wandering plant. |
| blackberry | clonal | Arching canes root where they touch ground. Most blackberries in a hedge are not separate plants. |
| birch | sexual | Catkins, wind-blown seed. A pioneer that lives fast and dies relatively young. |
| rowan | sexual | Flowers, then berries; birds take the seed far afield. Once planted by doorways against harm. |
| hazel | clonal | Cut to the ground, it sends up seven new stems. A single rootstock can live a thousand years. |
| hawthorn | sexual | Flowers in May, haws in autumn. A single hawthorn can stand four hundred years. |
| fern | spore | No flower, no seed. Reproduces by dust-like spores — a pattern unchanged for 300 million years. |
| foxglove | sexual | Biennial. The first year it waits low to the ground; the second it sends up a single spike and dies. |
| ash | sexual | Paired keys spiral down on the wind. An individual tree may flower male, female, or both — and switch between years. |
| oak | sexual | Wind-pollinated. Each oak flowers male and female on the same branches, weeks apart to avoid selfing. |
| bluebell | clonal | Spreads mostly by bulb — seed-grown bluebells take five years to flower, and most of a wood is one extended family. |
| wild_garlic | clonal | Spreads by bulb offsets and by seed. A carpet of it is often a single slow colony. |
| chanterelle | symbiotic | The visible mushroom is the fruit. The rest is woven through tree roots, trading sugar for phosphorus. |
| fly_agaric | symbiotic | The mushroom is brief. Its true body is a kilometres-wide network entangled with tree roots, trading sugar for minerals. |
| bracket_fungus | spore | Decomposer. Shelf-shaped fruiting bodies release trillions of spores to find the next dead tree. |
| earthball | spore | Splits open at maturity, releasing a brown cloud of spores into the wind. No stem, no gills — just a sac that bursts. |
| shaggy_inkcap | spore | Autodigests. The ripening cap dissolves itself into black ink, dripping spores in slow drops over hours rather than releasing them all at once. |
| stinkhorn | spore | Sidesteps the wind. The smell summons flies; the flies do the dispersal. An unusual partnership for a mushroom. |
| honey_fungus | clonal | A single honey fungus in Oregon covers nine square kilometres and is over two thousand years old. The caps are merely the visible flowers of one underground body. |
| deathcap | symbiotic | Mycorrhizal partner of mature oak and beech. The lethality is incidental — the toxin evolved against insects, not us. |
| holly | dioecious | Holly plants are male or female. A lone holly bears no berries. |
| ivy | opportunist | Climbs without parasitising. Flowers last, when little else does. Roots wherever the stem touches. |
